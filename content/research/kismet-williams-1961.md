## Identity and versions

**Kismet** — Williams Electronic Manufacturing Corp., **1961**, model **258**, four-player electromechanical game. Released in November 1961; approximately **700 produced**. Modern archives credit **Steve Kordek** with design and **George Molentin** with artwork. The playfield has two short-era flippers, three pop bumpers, four passive bumpers, two slingshots and a mechanically **moving center target**. [S1][S3] ([Kineticist][1])

Operator-adjustable **3- or 5-ball** play is confirmed by Williams' contemporary flyer and launch coverage. There are no software versions, editions or multiballs. [S1][S2] ([Museum of the Game][2])

## Thirty-second game plan

1. **Build the circular light progression each ball.** Williams explicitly says advancing the circle increases the game's “high score potential”; importantly, the circle **resets every ball**. [S1][S2]
2. Keep the ball working the **upper bumper area** to improve those lit scoring states. Experienced play reports describe this as the setup phase before going for maximum-value shots. **Observed strategy.** [S4] ([Pinside][3])
3. When ready, attack the **moving center target**: a hit scores **50 or 100 points**, while a miss scores only **1**. In a game where 100 is enormous, timing the target is the primary cash-out. [S1][S2]
4. **Ahead:** favor survival and upper-playfield accumulation. **Behind:** accept the center-target risk and chase 100-point hits.
5. Alternate payoff: the side drains/outlanes can themselves become very valuable; experienced players specifically describe **100-point outlanes**, creating the bizarre choice between preserving the ball and taking a large terminal score. [S4]

## Core rules and persistence

**Confirmed:** the central target mechanically moves across the playfield. A successful hit is worth **50 or 100**, while a shot that reaches the mechanism but misses the target scores **1 point**. [S1][S2] 

A circular bank of playfield lamps advances during play, and Williams says progressing it raises the player's **scoring potential**. That progression **resets at the start of every ball**; it is not a game-long build. [S1][S2]

Experienced-player evidence says the practical route is to keep the upper bumpers active until the playfield's higher-value states are lit, then exploit the center and side scoring. [S4] The available manufacturer flyer does **not** provide a reliable switch-by-switch table connecting each circle position to every bumper/lane value, so those exact steps should not be invented.

There is no end-of-ball bonus, multiplier, lock or mode persistence comparable to later games. The leverage is instead **within-ball escalation followed by large 50/100-point shots**.

## Skill shots

**None found as a plunge-based skill shot.**

Period Williams advertising calls the moving center target **“a real skill shot”**, but this means a timed flipper shot at the moving mechanism—not a modern plunge/launch skill-shot rule. [S1]

That distinction is worth preserving on commentary: “skill shot” was being used descriptively by Williams in 1961.

## Secondary features

No ball save, kickback, Extra Ball, video/display mode, mystery award or player-controlled utility was verified.

Williams advertised a **two-way match** and adjustable 3/5-ball play. Match awards are replay mechanics rather than meaningful tournament scoring features. [S1][S2]

## What to watch

* **Circular lamps:** these are the clearest game-state indicator. More progress means higher scoring potential, but they reset next ball. [S1]
* **Moving target position:** experienced players report that its movement includes a noticeable pause on one side, making the shot genuinely timeable rather than purely random. [S4] ([Pinside][4])
* **Ball trapped among upper bumpers:** usually good news; this is where the player wants switch activity while building the current ball's scoring state. [S4]
* **Lit/high-value side lane or outlane:** a potential 100-point score may make an otherwise disastrous-looking exit surprisingly consequential. [S4]

## Match strategy

**Playing ahead:** keep the ball upstairs and take the game's incremental scoring rather than repeatedly challenging the center mechanism. With no carryover of the light circle, the objective is simply to turn the current ball into a long productive one. This is a **strategic inference** supported by the confirmed reset rule and observed-player descriptions. [S1][S4]

**Playing behind:** attack the moving target when its timing is favorable. A **100-point hit is enormous** relative to ordinary scoring, and several successful shots can erase a meaningful deficit quickly. Accepting a valuable lit outlane rather than desperately trying to save an already-lost ball can also make sense. [S4]

**Key recurring decision:** **farm the upper playfield or shoot center now?** Kismet's entire competitive tension is concentrated there: progressive safer setup versus a deliberately dangerous 50/100-point cash-out.

## Danger zones

* **Huge center gap:** contemporary players repeatedly emphasize that the short flippers feel extremely far apart; center returns are brutal. [S4][S5] ([SteveChannel][5])
* **Moving-target miss:** gives only 1 point and can send the ball straight back into the lower danger area.
* **Target rebound:** even a successful center shot sacrifices much of the control gained by staying in the upper playfield.
* **Bumper exits:** useful for scoring setup but inherently uncontrolled and capable of finding either outlane.
* **Outlanes:** unusually high-scoring when qualified/reported at 100, but the payoff is terminal; a commentator should not mistake the score jump for a save. [S4]

## Spoken commentary cues

* “That's the whole game: build the lights upstairs, then decide when to challenge the moving target.”
* “A clean target hit can be fifty or a hundred; a miss is basically worthless at one point.”
* “Watch the target timing—it actually pauses during its travel, so this isn't completely random.”
* “One hundred points is huge on Kismet; that's a legitimate match-moving shot.”
* “The strange part is the outlane can be worth a pile of points—the score can jump while the ball is draining.”

## Trivia

* Kismet is a **four-player** EM from an era when many comparable Williams games were still one- or two-player; its backbox therefore contains four mechanical-reel score sets. [S1][S3]
* Williams built only about **700** examples. [S3]
* The moving-target concept followed Williams' **Magic Clock**, introduced at the end of 1960 and documented as the first pinball machine to use such a target. [S6] ([Kineticist][6])
* Contemporary Williams advertising explicitly sold the mechanism on **timing and aiming skill**, not randomness. [S1][S2]
* The title/art package is commonly associated with the **1953 Broadway musical and 1955 film *Kismet***, using an Arabian Nights/Baghdad visual motif. [S3] ([Kineticist][7])

### Uncertainties and conflicts

* **Circle progression:** Williams confirms that advancing the circular lights raises scoring potential and that they reset each ball, but the available primary material does not specify the complete switch/value mapping. [S1][S2]
* **50 versus 100 target state:** both values are manufacturer-confirmed, but I did not find authoritative documentation establishing exactly which target position or lamp state produces each. [S1][S2]
* **Outlane scoring:** an experienced player who repeatedly played the Lyons Classic Pinball example states that the outlanes are worth **100 points** [S4], but the manufacturer flyer available online does not spell out whether this is unconditional or lamp-dependent.
* **Design credit:** current databases credit Steve Kordek [S3], although the Strong Museum's surviving Williams playfield-design finding aid lists the #258 Kismet drawing itself as **“unidentified.”** This is an archival attribution gap, not strong evidence that Kordek's modern credit is wrong. [S7] ([The Strong National Museum of Play][8])
